# 2. Market Structure

## Bullish / Bearish Structure

SMC/ICT terminology varies between educators. In this revision, the following terms are used consistently:

- **Change of Character (CHoCH):** The first meaningful violation of a relevant swing point against the direction of the prior structure. A minor intrabar break of an insignificant swing should not automatically be treated as a CHoCH.
- **Market Structure Shift (MSS):** A stronger form of structural shift used as confirmation, normally supported by clear displacement through a relevant swing point and appropriate liquidity/context.
- **Break of Structure (BOS):** A continuation break in the direction of the established structure after the initial shift has been confirmed.

In a bullish transition, price violates a relevant swing high after forming a meaningful low. In a bearish transition, price violates a relevant swing low after forming a meaningful high.

![Market structure transition overview 1](../attachments/fig-02-01-structure-transition.png)
![Market structure transition overview 2](../attachments/fig-02-02-structure-transition.png)

## Bullish Structure

A bullish structure is established when price forms a meaningful low and then breaks a relevant prior swing high with convincing price delivery. A displacement move and a candle close through the level provide stronger confirmation than a wick-only breach.

In a bullish environment, traders may look for long opportunities when price retraces toward the broken swing, an Order Block, a Fair Value Gap, or another aligned PD Array, provided the higher-timeframe bias and risk plan support the trade.

![Bullish structure](../attachments/fig-02-03-bullish-structure.png)

## Bearish Structure

A bearish structure is established when price forms a meaningful high and then breaks a relevant prior swing low with convincing price delivery. A displacement move and a candle close through the level provide stronger confirmation than a wick-only breach.

In a bearish environment, traders may look for short opportunities when price retraces toward the broken swing, an Order Block, a Fair Value Gap, or another aligned PD Array, provided the higher-timeframe bias and risk plan support the trade.

![Bearish structure](../attachments/fig-02-04-bearish-structure.png)

# 3. SMC Entry Checklist

- Identify a potential **Liquidity Grab / Liquidity Sweep** (PDH/PDL, London Highs/Lows, Equal Highs/Lows, or other relevant external liquidity).
- Look for a break of a **relevant** high/low with clear **Displacement**, producing an MSS/CHoCH rather than a minor swing violation.
- Identify a valid **Order Block / Fair Value Gap / Breaker Block** created or confirmed by the displacement.
- Wait for price to return to the selected PD Array and assess whether the higher-timeframe bias and liquidity target remain aligned.
- Define invalidation, stop placement, target, position size, and **Risk-to-Reward (R:R)** before entry. If following the author's original filter, 1:1 may be treated as a minimum threshold, but the strategy should ultimately be validated by historical expectancy rather than a fixed R:R alone.

# 4. Liquidity

## Identification of Potential Liquidity Pools

- Previous Day / Week / Month Highs and Lows
- Equal Highs / Equal Lows
- London Session Highs / Lows
- Other obvious market-session highs and lows
- Clearly visible swing highs / lows where stop or breakout orders may cluster

## Why These Levels Matter

Within the SMC/ICT framework, obvious highs and lows are treated as **potential liquidity pools** because stop-loss orders, breakout orders, and other resting orders may cluster around them.

- **Stop-Loss Orders:** Traders often place protective stops beyond recent swing highs or lows.
- **Breakout Orders:** Buy stops may sit above resistance and sell stops below support.
- **Liquidity for Larger Orders:** Larger participants generally need sufficient opposing liquidity to enter or exit size efficiently. However, a chart alone cannot prove that a specific institution intentionally moved price to a level for this purpose; this should be treated as an interpretive model rather than an observable fact.

## When Price Approaches a Potential Liquidity Pool

- Price may accelerate into the level as resting orders are triggered.
- A brief move through the level may form a **liquidity sweep**.
- A sweep does **not** guarantee reversal. Price may reverse, consolidate, or continue through the level depending on higher-timeframe structure, displacement, order flow, and subsequent confirmation.

## Trading Liquidity Sweeps

- **Anticipation:** Mark likely liquidity pools in advance using market structure and higher-timeframe context.
- **Confirmation:** Rather than assuming every sweep will reverse, look for a meaningful CHoCH/MSS, displacement, and a valid PD Array after the sweep.
- **Execution:** Entries can be structured around the confirmed reversal or continuation scenario, with invalidation and targets defined before the trade.

# 5. Order Blocks

## Bullish Order Block

In SMC/ICT terminology, a **Bullish Order Block (OB)** is commonly identified as the last bearish candle, or a tightly related opposing candle cluster, before a meaningful bullish displacement that produces a clear structural consequence. It is interpreted as a price zone from which aggressive buying was delivered; the chart itself does not independently prove the identity or intent of the participants involved.

### Characteristics

- **Location:** Can form near a significant low in a reversal setup or within an established bullish structure as a continuation setup; it is not limited to the absolute bottom of a downtrend.
- **Formation:** Usually the last opposing bearish candle before strong bullish displacement.
- **Validation:** Stronger when the move from the OB displaces through a relevant swing, aligns with higher-timeframe bias, and leaves a clear liquidity target.
- **Trading Strategy:** Traders may look for a return to the OB and then assess reaction, structure, and invalidation before entering long.

## Bearish Order Block

In SMC/ICT terminology, a **Bearish Order Block (OB)** is commonly identified as the last bullish candle, or a tightly related opposing candle cluster, before a meaningful bearish displacement that produces a clear structural consequence. It is interpreted as a price zone from which aggressive selling was delivered.

### Characteristics

- **Location:** Can form near a significant high in a reversal setup or within an established bearish structure as a continuation setup; it is not limited to the absolute top of an uptrend.
- **Formation:** Usually the last opposing bullish candle before strong bearish displacement.
- **Validation:** Stronger when the move from the OB displaces through a relevant swing, aligns with higher-timeframe bias, and leaves a clear liquidity target.
- **Trading Strategy:** Traders may look for a return to the OB and then assess reaction, structure, and invalidation before entering short.

# 6. Breaker Blocks

## Explanation of Breaker Blocks

A **Breaker Block** is not simply a consolidation zone that later becomes support or resistance. In ICT/SMC terminology, a Breaker Block is a **failed Order Block that flips polarity after structural failure**.

- A **Bullish Breaker** typically develops when a Bearish Order Block fails, price trades decisively above it with bullish displacement/structural confirmation, and the former bearish zone later acts as support on a retracement.
- A **Bearish Breaker** typically develops when a Bullish Order Block fails, price trades decisively below it with bearish displacement/structural confirmation, and the former bullish zone later acts as resistance on a retracement.

## Formation

A higher-quality Breaker Block generally contains the following sequence:

1. A valid Order Block forms.
2. The Order Block fails to hold.
3. Price breaks through the zone with meaningful displacement and a structural shift/continuation in the new direction.
4. Price later retraces to the failed OB zone.
5. The zone is evaluated as a potential Breaker in its new support/resistance role.

## Identification

Look for:

- A clearly defined prior Order Block.
- A decisive violation of that Order Block rather than a minor wick through it.
- A meaningful shift in structure and/or displacement confirming the new direction.
- A return to the same failed OB zone from the opposite side.

## Usage in Trading

- **Polarity Flip:** The key idea is role reversal: former support becomes resistance, or former resistance becomes support.
- **Entry Context:** The retest of the Breaker may be used as an entry location when it aligns with higher-timeframe bias, liquidity objectives, and a defined invalidation point.
- **Not a Standalone Signal:** A Breaker should not be treated as automatically valid simply because price revisits a prior zone.

# 7. Fair-Value Gaps

## Explanation of Fair-Value Gaps

A **Fair Value Gap (FVG)** in ICT/SMC is a **three-candle price-delivery imbalance**. It is defined by a lack of overlap between the wick of Candle 1 and the opposite wick of Candle 3, usually created around a strong displacement candle (Candle 2).

The zone is not literally an area where "no trading activity occurred"—Candle 2 traded through the prices. The concept is that the price was delivered one-sidedly, leaving a visible non-overlap between Candle 1 and Candle 3. In ICT/SMC this is interpreted as an inefficiency that price may later revisit; a revisit is not guaranteed.

## Characteristics of Fair-Value Gaps

### Bullish FVG

- Forms when **Candle 1 High < Candle 3 Low**.
- The FVG is the price range between Candle 1's high and Candle 3's low.
- Candle 2 usually shows strong bullish displacement.

### Bearish FVG

- Forms when **Candle 1 Low > Candle 3 High**.
- The FVG is the price range between Candle 3's high and Candle 1's low.
- Candle 2 usually shows strong bearish displacement.

### Significance

- Represents a visible imbalance in three-candle price delivery within the ICT/SMC framework.
- FVGs created by meaningful displacement, after a liquidity event, and in alignment with higher-timeframe bias are generally treated as more relevant than random non-overlaps.
- Price may partially fill, fully fill, ignore, or trade through an FVG; it should not be assumed that every FVG must be filled.

# 10. Top Down Analysis

## Start with Higher Time frames

- **Monthly and Weekly Charts:** These charts are used to identify the long-term trend and significant support and resistance levels. It's important to understand the broader market structure, including trends, ranges, and key levels that could influence price movements.

### Identify Key Levels and Zones

- **Support and Resistance:** Look for major support and resistance levels, supply and demand zones, and institutional levels on the higher time frames. These levels are often areas where price has reacted strongly in the past and are likely to do so again.
- **Market Structure:** Determine the overall market structure, such as higher highs and higher lows in an uptrend or lower highs and lower lows in a downtrend. This helps in understanding the prevailing market sentiment and potential future price movements.
![Higher timeframe key levels](../attachments/fig-10-01-higher-timeframe-levels.png)


## Refine Analysis on Lower Time frames

- **Daily and 4-Hour Charts:** Use these time frames to refine the analysis and look for confirmation of the levels identified on the higher time frames. These charts help in identifying more precise entry and exit points based on the overall market context.
- **Pattern Recognition:** Look for price action patterns, such as breakouts, retests, and consolidation phases, which align with the higher time frame analysis.
![Lower timeframe refinement](../attachments/fig-10-02-lower-timeframe-refinement.png)

## Execute on Even Lower Time frames

- **1-Hour and Lower Charts:** Once the higher time frames provide a clear context and potential trading zones, use lower time frames for precise entry and exit points. It's important to look for confluence between higher and lower time frames to increase the probability of successful trades.

# 11. Accumulation

## Wyckoff's Schematic

A Wyckoff **Accumulation Trading Range (TR)** develops after a decline as supply is absorbed and the market prepares for a potential Markup. The exact sequence varies; not every schematic contains every event.

- **Preliminary Support (PS):** Meaningful buying begins to slow the prior downtrend.
- **Selling Climax (SC):** Selling pressure reaches a climax, often with wide spread and elevated volume.
- **Automatic Rally (AR):** Selling pressure temporarily subsides and price rallies; the AR helps define the upper boundary of the TR.
- **Secondary Test (ST):** Price revisits the SC area to test whether supply has diminished. Multiple STs can occur.
- **Spring / Shakeout (optional):** Price moves below established support and then returns into the TR, testing remaining supply. A Spring is **not required**; some valid accumulation structures form without one.
- **Test:** Price tests the Spring/shakeout or another area of potential supply, ideally with reduced selling pressure.
- **Sign of Strength (SOS):** A strong advance showing increasing demand and the market's ability to move toward or through resistance.
- **Last Point of Support (LPS) / Back-Up (BU):** A reaction after an SOS that holds support and can confirm that demand is dominating supply.
- **Phase E / Markup:** Price leaves the accumulation TR and the uptrend becomes established.

![Wyckoff accumulation schematic](../attachments/fig-11-01-wyckoff-accumulation-schematic.png)

# 12. Distribution

## Wyckoff's Schematic

A Wyckoff **Distribution Trading Range (TR)** develops after an advance as supply begins to overcome demand and the market prepares for a potential Markdown. It is not an accumulation schematic with the labels left unchanged.

- **Preliminary Supply (PSY):** Meaningful supply begins to appear after a pronounced advance, slowing the uptrend.
- **Buying Climax (BC):** Buying pressure reaches a climax as heavy public demand is met by significant supply near a potential top.
- **Automatic Reaction (AR):** With buying pressure diminished and supply active, price reacts downward; the AR helps define the lower boundary of the TR.
- **Secondary Test (ST):** Price revisits the BC/resistance area to test the balance between demand and supply. Multiple STs can occur.
- **Upthrust (UT):** A temporary move above resistance that fails and returns into the range.
- **Upthrust After Distribution (UTAD, optional):** A late-stage false breakout above resistance that tests remaining demand. A UTAD is the distributional counterpart to a Spring, but it is **not required** in every distribution structure.
- **Sign of Weakness (SOW):** A decline toward or through support showing that supply is gaining dominance.
- **Last Point of Supply (LPSY):** A weak rally after a SOW that fails to regain prior resistance and may provide a final area of supply before Markdown.
- **Phase E / Markdown:** Price leaves the distribution TR and the downtrend becomes established.

![Wyckoff distribution schematic](../attachments/fig-12-01-wyckoff-distribution-schematic.png)

# 13. Second Step Accumulation — Accumulation Completion / Markup Transition

The original chapter incorrectly called the later part of the same initial accumulation structure **Reaccumulation**. In Wyckoff terminology, **Reaccumulation** normally refers to a new trading range that forms later within an already established uptrend. It is not simply the second half of the original bottoming range.

After the initial **PS → SC → AR → ST** sequence, completion of the same accumulation TR may involve:

- **Phase B Development:** Price continues to trade within the range while supply is absorbed and cause is built.
- **Spring / Shakeout and Test (optional):** Price may briefly break support and recover, followed by a test. This event is not mandatory.
- **Sign of Strength (SOS):** Price advances with convincing demand and challenges or breaks range resistance.
- **Last Point of Support (LPS) / Back-Up:** Price pulls back after strength while holding above or near former resistance/support, providing evidence that demand remains dominant.
- **Markup Transition:** After the TR is completed, price exits the range and the bullish trend continues.

### Distinguishing Reaccumulation

A **Reaccumulation** is a later consolidation/trading range that forms **after Markup has already begun**, often acting as a stepping stone before the existing uptrend resumes.

![Second step accumulation and reaccumulation](../attachments/fig-13-01-second-step-accumulation.png)

# 14. Second Step Distribution — Distribution Completion / Markdown Transition

The original chapter copied the accumulation chapter and used accumulation-side terms such as **PS/SC/AR/ST**, **Reaccumulation**, and **accumulation range**. These do not correctly describe the completion of an initial distribution structure.

After the initial **PSY → BC → AR → ST** sequence, completion of the same distribution TR may involve:

- **Upthrust / UTAD (optional):** Price may briefly move above resistance and fail. A UTAD is a late-stage variant and is not required in every distribution.
- **Sign of Weakness (SOW):** Price declines toward or through the lower boundary of the range with increasing evidence of supply.
- **Last Point of Supply (LPSY):** A weak rally after a SOW fails to return to prior resistance, showing exhausted demand and persistent supply.
- **Breakdown / Markdown Transition:** Price leaves the distribution range to the downside and the bearish trend begins or accelerates.
- **Retest:** A later reaction toward former support may be evaluated as resistance, but a retest is not guaranteed.

### Distinguishing Redistribution

A **Redistribution** is a later consolidation/trading range that forms **after Markdown is already underway**, often acting as a stepping stone before the existing downtrend resumes. It is not simply the second half of the original distribution top.

# 15. Defining Your Trading Range / Daily Bias

## Defining Your Trading Range

Previous Day High (PDH) and Previous Day Low (PDL) are useful reference levels for framing daily bias, external liquidity, and a potential dealing range. They should not be treated as guaranteed boundaries that price must remain between.

For Example:
![PDH/PDL trading range example 1](../attachments/fig-15-01-pdh-pdl-range-example-1.png)

If price briefly trades above PDH and then rejects back below it without sustained acceptance, PDH and PDL may continue to frame the day's working range. Confirmation should come from subsequent structure and price delivery rather than from the level alone.

If price breaks below PDL with strong displacement and begins to respect PDL as resistance on a retracement, that behavior can support a bearish continuation bias. Conversely, sustained acceptance above PDH can invalidate a range-bound assumption and shift the working range higher.

Using PDH and PDL helps define likely liquidity objectives and contextual boundaries, but the market may expand beyond either level.

# 17. Risk Management

## Understanding Market Structure

Understanding market structure before entering a trade is essential. Relevant support/resistance, liquidity, trend condition, volatility, and structural invalidation should be identified before risk is committed. Market structure improves the quality of a risk plan, but it does not make future price movement certain.

## Risk-Reward Ratio and Expectancy

Risk-to-Reward (R:R) is useful, but a fixed 1:2 or 1:3 target is not a universal requirement. A trading system should be evaluated using its **historical win rate, average win, average loss, trading costs, and expectancy**.

A simplified expectancy formula is:

`Expectancy = (Win Rate × Average Win) − (Loss Rate × Average Loss) − Trading Costs`

A strategy with a lower average R multiple can still be viable if its win rate is sufficiently high, while a large theoretical R:R does not guarantee profitability if the win rate is too low or execution costs are high.

## Position Sizing

Position size should be calculated from account equity, the distance to the structural invalidation/stop, and the maximum risk allowed by the tested trading plan. The objective is to prevent any single trade or losing sequence from causing unacceptable damage to trading capital.

## Stop Loss and Take Profit Orders

Stop-loss and take-profit orders are core risk-management tools. Stops should be placed where the trade thesis is invalidated, with market volatility and instrument behavior considered. Targets should be tied to realistic liquidity objectives, structure, and the strategy's tested exit logic rather than an arbitrary R multiple alone.

## Trade Management

Once a trade is active, management rules should be defined in advance. These may include moving a stop to break even, partial profit-taking, trailing stops, or holding to a fixed target. Frequent discretionary changes without a tested rule can alter the strategy's expectancy.

## Avoiding Over-Leverage

Leverage should be used cautiously. Excessive leverage magnifies both normal variance and execution errors and can turn a statistically ordinary losing sequence into an unacceptable drawdown.

## Psychological Discipline

Risk management also requires behavioral consistency. Follow the tested plan, avoid impulsive changes driven by fear or greed, and separate the quality of a decision from the outcome of any single trade.

## Backtesting and Strategy Development

Backtesting is necessary to estimate how a strategy behaved on historical data, including win rate, average R, drawdown, losing streaks, and regime sensitivity. Backtests do not guarantee future results, so forward testing and ongoing review are also important.

## Risk Assessment Tools

Volatility measures, market structure, historical adverse excursion, and liquidity conditions can help define more realistic stop distances and position sizes.

## Continuous Learning

Markets change. Effective risk management requires reviewing trades, updating assumptions when evidence changes, and adapting only when the data supports the change rather than reacting to a small number of outcomes.
